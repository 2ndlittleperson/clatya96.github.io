Will AI Image Synthesis Technology Help Constructivist Education at The Online Art Museum?

An online art museum that asks you to make something. Upload one of your own photos, borrow a Mucha painting’s style for it, and the result hangs in your own gallery at the end of the visit. We built it, then tested it against 83 visitors to see whether making changes the visit.

The system

Why we built it

COVID-19 pushed museums online faster than they had planned to go, and online education went with them. What mostly arrived was the gallery wall transplanted into a browser: high-resolution images, wall text beside them, a visitor who scrolls. That is a reasonable way to look at art and a poor way to learn it.

Constructivist education starts from the opposite assumption — that people build understanding by making things and tying new material to what they already have, not by receiving it. The question this project asked is whether AI image synthesis gives an online museum the one thing it had been missing to work that way: a cheap, immediate way for every visitor to produce something of their own inside the exhibition, out of material that is already theirs.

Three steps through the museum

01

Bring your own photograph

The visit opens by asking for a picture rather than offering one. A visitor uploads a photo from their own camera roll — a street, a café table, a friend — and picks the one they want to work with.

  • Starting from the visitor’s own material is the constructivist move: the new thing attaches to something they already care about.
  • It is also the manipulation the study turns on, since one condition hands visitors a photo instead of letting them bring one.

02

Borrow a painting’s style

A grid of Alphonse Mucha’s Art Nouveau posters stands in for the exhibition itself. Choosing one is choosing a style, and the system synthesizes the visitor’s photograph in it.

  • Looking closely at a painting becomes necessary rather than virtuous — you have to decide which one you want your own picture to look like.
  • The comparison is immediate: the source painting and the synthesized result sit on the same page.

03

Leave with a gallery

Each result is added to a personal gallery that builds up across the visit, so the museum ends with a small body of work that belongs to the visitor.

  • The output is the record of the visit, which is a different souvenir from a list of paintings you scrolled past.
  • It gives the session a shape: arrive, make, look at what you made.

How it was tested

Rather than argue the design, we ran it. Each participant visited exactly one of three web-based museums, which differed only in how much of the making they were allowed to do.

Participants
83 Between-subjects: one museum each, no comparison across conditions by the visitor
Conditions
Three online museums (1) no AI image synthesis · (2) synthesis applied to photos the museum supplied · (3) synthesis applied to photos the visitor uploaded themselves
Measures
Motivation, satisfaction, constructivist experience Self-reported after the visit

What we found

The self-uploaded condition came out ahead of both others: those visitors reported higher motivation, higher satisfaction, and an experience they described in more constructivist terms. Synthesis alone was not what did it — the condition that applied the same AI to museum-supplied photos did not produce the same effect.

That is the useful part of the result for anyone building one of these. The technology is not the active ingredient; whose material goes into it is. An online museum that generates impressive images for a visitor is doing something different from one that helps a visitor make an image of their own, and only the second reads as learning.
